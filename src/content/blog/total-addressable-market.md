If you're a founder or you lead sales, you will be asked how big your market is. Your total addressable market (TAM) is the answer: the yearly revenue you'd earn if every company that could buy from you did. This guide shows how to size it from a real count of companies, narrow it to the part you can win, and check the number before you put it in a plan or a pitch deck.

**Quick summary**

- TAM is the number of possible customers times what each one would pay you in a year.
- Count from the bottom up: write your ideal customer as filters, then count the companies that match.
- Narrow TAM to the market you can serve (SAM) and the share you can win soon (SOM).
- Check the count by looking at real companies in it before you trust it.

## What is total addressable market (TAM)?

Total addressable market is the total yearly revenue available to you if every company that fits your product bought it.

The simplest way to work it out is bottom-up:

- count the companies that could buy from you
- multiply by what one of them would pay you in a year

An example: if 4,000 companies fit and each would pay $12,000 a year, your TAM is $48 million a year. (These numbers are made up.)

The other way, top-down, starts from a big industry report figure and takes a slice of it. It's quick, but the slice is usually a guess. Bottom-up is slower and much easier to defend.

## How are TAM, SAM and SOM different?

They are three circles, each inside the last. TAM is everyone who could buy. SAM is the part you can serve today. SOM is the part you can realistically win in the next few years.

Investors look at all three. Your sales plan should be built on SOM.

## Why size your market at all?

Because the size of the market sets how big the company can get. Four reasons to do it properly:

- **You find out early if the market is too small.** A product with 200 possible customers needs a high price or a different market.
- **Fundraising goes faster.** A count you can explain, company by company, holds up when investors push on it.
- **Sales targets get real.** If SOM is 300 companies, a plan to win 1,000 next year is wrong before it starts.
- **You can compare segments.** Count two segments the same way and you see which one is worth going after first.

## How do you calculate TAM, step by step?

Five steps, from your best customers to a number you can defend.

### 1. Write your ideal customer as filters

Start from your ideal customer profile (ICP): industry, headcount, location, and the software they use. Turn each trait into a filter, like "Logistics, 50 to 1,000 employees, United States." If you don't have an ICP yet, our [ideal customer profile guide](/blog/ideal-customer-profile) walks through it.

### 2. Count the companies that match

This is the slow part to do by hand. With Pramaan, "[Size a market](/missions/market.size)" counts how many companies match your filters. It also counts the people who match a role, and estimates how many known work mailboxes there are, all without pulling any contact details.

The people count matters if you charge per seat or per user. Then your count of possible buyers is people, not companies.

### 3. Set a yearly value per customer

Use your own pricing. Take what a typical customer pays you in a year today. If you have no customers yet, use the price you plan to charge, and say so when you share the number.

### 4. Multiply, then narrow

Companies times yearly value gives you TAM. For SAM, keep only the regions, languages and segments you can sell to today and count again. For SOM, pick the share of SAM you could win in two or three years, based on your team's size and your current win rate. Write down why you picked that share.

### 5. Check the count against real companies

A count is only as good as its filters. "[Map the companies in a market](/missions/market.map)" lists companies in a segment, profiles each one and sorts them into named groups. Read through them. If half aren't real buyers, tighten a filter and count again.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **Starting from a report figure.** "Logistics is a $10 trillion industry" says nothing about who will buy your software. Count your own buyers.
- **Counting everyone in the industry.** If your filters let in companies you would never sell to, TAM looks bigger and means less.
- **Using a price you don't charge.** Base yearly value on real deals, or label it as planned.
- **Treating SOM as a promise.** It's a target based on a share you chose. Write down how you chose it.
- **Sizing once.** New regions, new products and new prices change the count. Recount when they change.

## Questions teams ask about TAM

**Is TAM measured in companies or in money?**
In money per year. The company count is the input; multiply it by yearly value to get TAM.

**What if I charge per seat?**
Count people in the roles that would use your product, not companies. [Size a market](/missions/market.size) counts both.

**Do I need a developer to use Pramaan for this?**
No. You ask in plain words, or your AI assistant asks for you.

**How precise is the count?**
It's an estimate from data sources, which is why step 5 has you read real companies in the market before you trust it.
