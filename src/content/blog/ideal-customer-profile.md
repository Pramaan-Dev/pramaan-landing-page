If you're a founder, a sales lead or you run marketing, an ideal customer profile (ICP) is the short list of traits your best customers share. Write it down and your team stops chasing everyone. This guide shows how to build one from your own customers, test it on real companies, and use it to decide who to contact next.

**Quick summary**

- An ICP describes the companies that buy from you, get results and stay.
- Build it from your 10 to 20 best customers: their industry, size, software, pain and the event that made them buy.
- Write it as eight or fewer yes-or-no checks, then test it on real companies.
- Use it to pick targets, rank outreach and see which visiting companies fit.

## What is an ideal customer profile?

An ideal customer profile describes the kind of company that gets the most from what you sell.

A good ICP picks out companies that:

- have the problem you solve
- can pay for the fix
- have a reason to act now
- will still be with you in a year

Here's an example for a company that sells scheduling software to clinics: "Private clinics in the US and UK with 3 to 20 locations, a front desk of at least 5 people, still booking by phone, and hiring for receptionists."

## Is an ICP the same as a buyer persona?

No. The ICP says which companies to go after. A buyer persona says which person inside that company you talk to.

## Why build an ICP at all?

Because time and budget are limited, and most companies will never buy from you. An ICP gives you four things:

- **Less wasted marketing.** You write for one kind of reader, so the content and ads fit.
- **Faster lead qualification.** A new lead either passes the checks or it doesn't. Nobody argues about it in the weekly meeting.
- **One target for every team.** Marketing, sales and founders chase the same companies, and skip the same bad-fit deals, even when a deal looks big.
- **A steadier pipeline.** When most leads fit, more of them close, so next quarter is easier to forecast.

## How do you build an ideal customer profile?

Build it from your real customers in five steps.

### 1. List your best customers

Pick the 10 to 20 customers you'd clone if you could. Look for the ones that started fast, pay the most, stayed longest and got the clearest results. Leave out the ones that took the most effort to keep.

### 2. Find what they share

Look at four kinds of traits:

- **Firmographics**: industry, headcount, location, revenue band. These are the basic facts about a company.
- **Technographics**: the software they run. A company on a tool yours plugs into is often an easier sale.
- **Pain**: the problem that made them look for you, in their words.
- **Triggers**: events that made them act, like new funding, a new leader, or hiring for a role.

With Pramaan you can ask for these facts instead of digging. "[See what technology a company uses](/missions/company.techstack)" reads the tools on a company's site. "[See what a company is hiring for](/missions/company.hiring.signal)" lists its open roles and what they suggest.

### 3. Write it as yes-or-no checks

Turn each trait into a check you can answer: "Has 50 to 500 employees." "Uses Shopify." "Raised money in the last 12 months." Keep it to eight checks or fewer. Any more and nobody uses it.

### 4. Test it on companies you know

Run the checks on a few current customers and a few deals you lost. Your customers should pass. The lost deals should fail somewhere useful.

"[Qualify a company against your criteria](/missions/lead.qualify)" does this for one company at a time. You give it up to eight written checks. Each comes back as met, not met or unknown, with what the answer relied on. For a batch, "[Score companies against your ideal customer](/missions/company.icp.score)" scores up to 100 companies at once.

### 5. Check the market is big enough

An ICP that matches 40 companies in the world is too narrow. "[Size a market](/missions/market.size)" counts how many companies and people match your filters, without pulling any contact details. If the count is tiny, loosen one check and count again.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **Making it too broad.** "B2B companies with over 10 employees" rules nobody out. If almost every company passes, it isn't a profile.
- **Looking only at company size.** Two companies with 200 staff can need very different things. Size helps, but pain, software and timing tell you more.
- **Building it from hopes.** Base it on the companies that buy and stay, using your customer data.
- **Writing it once.** Your best customers change when your product and prices change. Review it every quarter against the deals you won and lost.
- **Mixing it up with the persona.** Company traits go in the ICP. Job titles and goals go in the persona.

## Questions teams ask about ICPs

**How many customers do I need to build an ICP?**
Ten good ones is enough to start. If you have fewer, use your best deals in progress and revisit once you close more.

**Can I have more than one ICP?**
Yes, if you sell to clearly different markets. Keep each one separate, and start with the one that brings in the most revenue.

**Do I need a developer to use Pramaan for this?**
No. You ask in plain words, or your AI assistant asks for you.

**How often should I update my ICP?**
Once a quarter, or sooner after a big change to your product or prices.
