If you run sales or marketing, lead scoring is how you decide which leads get a call today and which wait. This guide shows how to score leads on two things, fit and timing, and how to keep the score honest as your market changes.

**Quick summary**

- Lead scoring ranks leads so your team spends time on the ones most likely to buy.
- Score two things: fit (are they the right kind of company?) and timing (is something happening there now?).
- Build fit from your ideal customer profile, and timing from dated buying signals.
- Turn the scores into three tiers, and check them against won and lost deals every quarter.

## What is lead scoring?

Lead scoring gives each lead a rank, so your team knows who to contact first.

Most scores mix two questions:

- **Fit**: does this company look like the ones that buy from you and stay? Industry, size, location and software all count.
- **Timing**: is there a reason they'd buy now? A new round, a new leader or a hiring push all count.

Here's an example: "A 200-person logistics company in Texas, on the software we plug into, that just hired a new head of operations." High fit, good timing. That lead goes to the top.

## Is lead scoring the same as lead qualification?

No. Scoring ranks a whole list against each other. Qualification checks one lead against your rules and says yes, no or not sure.

## Why does lead scoring matter?

Because reps have limited hours, and most leads will never buy.

- **Reps call the best leads first.** The morning list is in order, so nobody picks at random.
- **Sales and marketing agree on what a good lead is.** The rules are written down, so the weekly argument gets shorter.
- **Good leads don't go cold.** A high-fit company with a fresh signal gets seen the same day.
- **You learn what works.** When you compare scores with won and lost deals, you see which traits matter.

## How do you build a lead score?

In six steps, starting from the customers you already have.

### 1. Start from your ideal customer profile

Your score is only as good as your idea of a good customer. If you don't have one yet, build it first with our [ideal customer profile guide](/blog/ideal-customer-profile). It gives you the fit traits and the buying triggers you'll use below.

### 2. Score fit for the whole list

Write your fit rules in plain words: "logistics or wholesale, 50 to 1,000 staff, based in the US." "[Score companies against your ideal customer](/missions/company.icp.score)" scores a batch of companies against those rules. Each one comes back with a fit probability and the profile it was based on, plus a note on what the explanation can't tell you.

### 3. Check the top leads one by one

Before a rep spends an hour on a lead, check it properly. "[Qualify a company against your criteria](/missions/lead.qualify)" takes up to eight written checks for one company. Each comes back as met, not met or unknown, with what the answer relied on. An "unknown" tells you what to ask on the first call.

### 4. Add timing from buying signals

Fit tells you who. Timing tells you when. "[Find companies showing buying signals](/missions/company.signals.find)" finds companies with dated events and checks each against your ideal customer.

To keep timing current, "[Set up a company signal watch](/missions/account.watch.setup)" watches your target companies for the events you choose. It needs approval. Then "[Review signals on target companies](/missions/account.signals.review)" gives you one list of what the watches found, ordered by score.

### 5. Combine fit and timing into tiers

Pramaan gives you the pieces: a fit result for each company and a ranked list of fresh signals. You decide how they add up. A simple rule works better than a clever one:

- **Tier A**: strong fit and a signal in the last 30 days. Call this week.
- **Tier B**: strong fit, no recent signal. Nurture and watch.
- **Tier C**: weak fit. Leave it, whatever the signal.

### 6. Check the score every quarter

Pull your won and lost deals from the last quarter. Did the won deals score high? Did the lost ones score low? If not, change one rule at a time and check again.

In this example, 60 points or more is Tier A, 35 to 59 is Tier B, and anything under 35 is Tier C. Keep the minus row. It stops a big but unwinnable account from floating to the top.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **Too many rules.** Thirty rules means nobody knows why a lead scored high. Start with six to eight.
- **Scoring only fit.** A perfect-fit company with no reason to change can sit in your pipeline for a year.
- **Scoring only activity.** Lots of page views from a bad-fit company is still a bad-fit company.
- **Never checking the score.** If you don't compare scores with real deals, you don't know if the score works.
- **Hiding the reasons.** Reps trust a score they can read. Show which rules a lead passed.
