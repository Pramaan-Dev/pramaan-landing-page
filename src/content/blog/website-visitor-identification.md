If you run marketing or sales, most of the companies reading your pricing page never fill in a form. Website visitor identification tells you which companies those visitors work for. This guide explains how it works, what it can and can't tell you, and how to turn a list of visiting companies into a short list your sales team can act on.

**Quick summary**

- Visitor identification matches the network address of a visit to the company that owns it. It names companies, not people.
- Visits from home internet and phone networks usually match nothing, so expect gaps.
- A visit is a hint. Check each company against your ideal customer profile before anyone acts.
- The useful output is short: a few fitting companies, the pages they read, and a next step for each.

## What is website visitor identification?

Website visitor identification is a way to see which companies are visiting your website, even when nobody fills in a form.

Every visit comes from an IP address, the number a network uses to reach the internet. Many companies run their own networks, so an address can often be traced back to the business behind it. Visitor identification looks up those addresses and returns company names, with facts like industry, size and location.

An example: a visit to your pricing page from an address owned by a 300-person logistics company in Ohio shows up as "a logistics company, 300 staff, read pricing twice this week." It doesn't say who read it.

## How is it different from web analytics?

Web analytics counts visits. Visitor identification puts a company name on some of them.

## Why does it matter?

Because most buyers research quietly before they talk to anyone. Four reasons to look:

- **You see interest earlier.** A company reading your pricing page three times is further along than a cold account.
- **Sales gets a reason to call.** "You were reading about our integrations" beats a generic opener, as long as you say it carefully.
- **Marketing sees who its content reaches.** If your blog draws students and your pricing page draws enterprises, that changes what you write next.
- **You can check your ideal customer profile.** If the companies that visit look nothing like the ones you target, one of them is wrong.

## How does website visitor identification work, step by step?

It takes five steps, from raw visits to a short list.

### 1. Pick the pages that matter

Not every visit means something. A company reading one blog post is curious. A company on your pricing, demo or comparison pages is closer to buying. Start with those pages.

### 2. Collect the visit addresses

Your site's server logs, or your hosting provider, record the IP address of each visit. Most analytics tools hide it, so take it from the logs. Pull the addresses for the pages you picked, over a set period such as the last seven days. A developer or whoever runs your site can export them once and set up a weekly export.

### 3. Match the addresses to companies

This is where Pramaan does the work. "[Identify companies visiting your site](/missions/company.visitors.identify)" looks up each address, finds the company behind it and fills in its profile. It resolves companies only. It never names the person who visited.

Some addresses won't match. Visits from home broadband and mobile networks belong to the internet provider, so they usually return nothing. That's normal, and it means a remote worker browsing from home won't show up.

### 4. Keep the companies that fit

The same mission then checks each company against your ideal customer criteria and keeps the confirmed fits. If you haven't written those criteria yet, start with our guide to the [ideal customer profile (ICP)](/blog/ideal-customer-profile).

For a closer look at one company, "[Qualify a company against your criteria](/missions/lead.qualify)" checks it against up to eight written criteria. Each one comes back met, not met or unknown, with the evidence it used.

### 5. Decide the next step for each company

A fitting company that read your pricing page twice is worth a call this week. A fitting company that read one blog post goes into a nurture campaign. A poor fit gets dropped, however big it is.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **Expecting every visit to match.** Home and mobile networks usually return nothing. Judge the tool by the fitting companies it finds, not the share of visits it names.
- **Treating one visit as buying intent.** One page view is a weak signal. Look for repeat visits to pages that matter.
- **Opening with "I saw you on our site."** It can feel like being watched. Lead with the problem the pages cover.
- **Skipping the fit check.** A big company that doesn't fit your ICP still won't buy. Filter first, then act.
- **Counting yourself.** Your own team, your agencies and your rivals visit too. Leave them out of the report.

## Questions teams ask about visitor identification

**Does it tell me who visited?**
No. It names the company behind a visit, never the person.

**Why do so many visits come back empty?**
Visits from home internet and phone networks belong to the internet provider, not an employer, so they usually match no company.

**Do I need a developer?**
Someone needs to export the visit addresses from your site once. After that, you ask in plain words, or your AI assistant asks for you.

**How often should we run it?**
Weekly works for most teams. It's often enough to call while interest is fresh, and few enough to read every row.
